A critical, evidence-led review of quantum computing, quantum sensing, post-quantum security, AI hardware and hybrid hospital infrastructure, with an adoption roadmap for 2026–2046.

Abstract

Quantum technologies are increasingly presented as potential enablers of healthcare innovation in molecular modelling, biomedical sensing, clinical optimisation and cybersecurity. Yet “quantum healthcare” groups together technologies at markedly different stages of scientific and commercial maturity. Artificial intelligence and high-performance computing already support selected hospital and biomedical workflows; fault-tolerant quantum computing remains a long-term engineering objective. Quantum sensing, particularly optically pumped magnetometry for magnetoencephalography, offers a more credible near- to medium-term clinical research pathway.

This review examines quantum computing, sensing, communication, post-quantum security and quantum-inspired computation. It evaluates applications in drug discovery, precision medicine, medical imaging, radiotherapy planning, logistics and health-data protection while separating established capabilities from emerging, experimental and speculative proposals. The central conclusion is that healthcare should pursue heterogeneous architectures combining electronic health records, edge systems, AI accelerators, HPC, cloud infrastructure and narrowly scoped quantum resources. Deployment should follow comparative validation, regulatory review, cybersecurity assessment and evidence of measurable patient, scientific or operational benefit.

Executive Summary

Strategic conclusion: hospitals should not treat quantum technology as a wholesale replacement for conventional computing. The credible pathway is a staged portfolio.

  • CORE: interoperable data, AI, HPC, cybersecurity, governance and workforce capability.
  • PILOT: post-quantum migration, OPM research and quantum-inspired optimisation.
  • RESEARCH: cloud-based quantum chemistry, quantum machine learning and experimental hybrid algorithms.

Immediate

Build cryptographic inventories, crypto-agility and post-quantum migration plans for long-lived health information.

Emerging

Evaluate OPM-MEG, quantum-safe networking and quantum-inspired operational research through controlled pilots.

Long-term

Use cloud or shared quantum resources for molecular simulation and specialised optimisation when rigorous classical benchmarks exist.

“The near-term quantum priority for most hospitals is not purchasing a quantum computer; it is preparing their information systems for post-quantum security.”

1. Introduction

Healthcare is becoming more computationally intensive. Precision medicine combines genomic, clinical, behavioural and environmental data; radiology and pathology generate high-resolution images at scale; intensive-care units produce continuous physiological time series; and managers coordinate beds, staff, medicines, equipment and patient journeys under uncertainty. At the same time, genomic, psychiatric, reproductive and paediatric records may need confidentiality for decades.

Quantum technologies may introduce new approaches to molecular simulation, selected optimisation, weak-signal measurement, secure communication and hybrid scientific computing. These possibilities do not share the same maturity. Quantum sensing may enter specialist research environments before fault-tolerant computation, while post-quantum cryptography can be deployed on conventional systems today.

Research question: How can quantum technologies contribute to healthcare research and hospital systems without overstating present maturity or underestimating clinical, economic, regulatory and governance requirements?

2. Research Objectives

  1. Explain the principal branches of quantum technology in healthcare-relevant language.
  2. Assess current and potential applications in medicine and hospital operations.
  3. Distinguish established, emerging, experimental and speculative applications.
  4. Evaluate infrastructure, cybersecurity, governance and workforce requirements.
  5. Examine national quantum programmes and shared research models.
  6. Propose an evidence-based adoption framework for hospitals and academic medical centres.

4. Quantum Technology Foundations

4.1 Quantum computing

Classical bits hold zero or one. Qubits are described by quantum states that may combine basis states until measurement. Superposition, entanglement and interference enable non-classical computation, but a quantum computer does not simply “try every answer simultaneously”. Algorithms must manipulate amplitudes so useful outcomes become more likely.

Major architectures include superconducting qubits, trapped ions, photonics, neutral atoms and quantum annealing. Assessment should consider physical and logical qubits, gate fidelity, connectivity, coherence, circuit depth, measurement accuracy, logical error rate, workload completion time, cost and performance against the strongest classical baseline.

4.2 Noise and error correction

Current processors are affected by noise, imperfect control and measurement errors. Quantum error correction encodes logical information across physical qubits. The overhead varies with hardware error rates, code choice, connectivity, algorithm and required reliability, which is why physical-qubit totals alone are inadequate.

4.3 Sensing, communication and security

Quantum sensing uses controlled quantum systems to measure magnetic fields, acceleration, gravity, time or temperature. Healthcare-relevant systems include SQUIDs, optically pumped magnetometers, diamond nitrogen-vacancy centres and quantum-enhanced optical sensors. Quantum key distribution can reveal interception under defined assumptions, but it does not secure endpoints, authentication, software or supply chains.

Post-quantum cryptography uses classical algorithms intended to withstand conventional and future quantum attacks. Key standards include ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA. Quantum-inspired methods, including tensor networks, digital annealing, Ising-model optimisation and specialised heuristics, run on classical hardware and must still prove superiority empirically.

5. Quantum Computing in Drug Discovery

Molecular behaviour is quantum mechanical, and electronic-structure calculations can become demanding as molecular size and interaction complexity increase. Candidate uses include ground-state and excited-state estimation, reaction pathways, protein-ligand analysis, structure generation and selected screening stages. The Variational Quantum Eigensolver is a prominent hybrid approach in which a classical optimiser updates parameters and a QPU estimates properties of a parameterised state.

Drug discovery also requires toxicity, solubility, absorption, distribution, metabolism, pharmacodynamics, manufacturing, dosage and clinical efficacy. A small molecular calculation is not equivalent to discovering a clinically useful medicine.

  1. Classically screen a broad chemical library.
  2. Identify a computationally difficult, narrowly defined property.
  3. Run hybrid quantum-classical estimation.
  4. Compare with established computational chemistry methods.
  5. Validate through synthesis and laboratory testing.
  6. Proceed through preclinical and clinical evaluation.

Value exists only if accuracy, speed, cost, energy use or scientific insight improves against a relevant baseline.

6. Genomics and Precision Medicine

Proposed applications include genomic classification, feature selection, sequence analysis, polygenic-risk modelling, patient stratification, biomarker discovery, trial matching and treatment optimisation. Classical genomics already benefits from sequencing platforms, distributed databases, cloud computing, GPU acceleration, bioinformatics pipelines, statistics and deep learning. Matching a classical model on a reduced dataset does not establish quantum advantage.

Studies should disclose dataset size and composition, preprocessing, dimensionality reduction, leakage controls, train/test separation, baselines, confidence intervals, subgroup performance, calibration, execution cost and reproducibility. Ethical governance and interpretability remain material requirements.

7. Quantum Sensing and Medical Imaging

7.1 OPM-MEG

Magnetoencephalography measures magnetic fields generated by neuronal activity. Conventional systems commonly use cryogenic SQUID sensors. Optically pumped magnetometers can operate without liquid-helium cooling and sit closer to the scalp, potentially enabling wearable arrangements, movement studies, paediatric research and adaptable placement.

Applications under investigation include epilepsy, movement disorders, dementia, schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental conditions. Constraints include environmental interference, shielding, calibration drift, cross-talk, sensitivity to deep sources, motion artefacts, multicentre standardisation, regulatory approval and reimbursement.

7.2 Magnetocardiography and quantum optical imaging

Magnetocardiography may support fetal or arrhythmia research but must outperform ECG, echocardiography, Holter monitoring, CT or MRI on clinically meaningful dimensions. Entangled photons, squeezed light and quantum correlations may assist low-light or sensitivity-limited imaging, but most biomedical uses remain experimental. Reports should specify photon dose, signal-to-noise ratio, spatial resolution, acquisition time, tissue depth, sample size and standard-of-care comparison.

7.3 Quantum dots

Quantum dots are nanoscale semiconductor materials used in fluorescence imaging, biosensors, assays and experimental delivery research. They are quantum-engineered materials, not quantum computers.

8. Therapeutics and Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy planning is a constrained optimisation problem involving dose coverage, organ protection, machine parameters and delivery. Quantum annealing and gate-based methods may be explored for beam angles, scheduling, dose distribution and brachytherapy, but must be benchmarked against mixed-integer programming, constraint programming, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, tabu search, IBM CPLEX, Gurobi and specialist planning systems. Clinical acceptability requires safety, reproducibility and dose-constraint compliance.

9. Hospital Logistics

Potential optimisation targets include staff rosters, theatres, beds, ambulance routing, medicine inventory, blood products, transplantation, diagnostic equipment and emergency queues. Barriers include translating operational rules into constraints, emergency changes, system integration, explainability, scheduling fairness and consistent reliability. Quantum-inspired optimisation is the more practical near-term option because it can run on conventional infrastructure.

10. AI and Quantum Hardware

Modern hospitals may use Intel Xeon or AMD EPYC processors, NVIDIA or AMD GPUs, cloud TPUs, FPGAs, embedded NPUs, HPC clusters, edge gateways and hybrid cloud. GPUs, TPUs, NPUs and FPGAs are classical accelerators; QPUs manipulate quantum states. Tensor Cores are specialised units in certain NVIDIA GPU architectures, not a synonym for NPUs.

“A qubit count is a hardware characteristic, not a healthcare outcome.”

12. Future Large-Hospital Model

Between 2035 and 2045, a major academic medical centre may connect four environments: a clinical-intelligence environment for regulated AI; a research environment joining GPUs, national supercomputing and cloud QPUs; an advanced-sensing environment for wearable magnetometry and biomarker research; and a quantum-safe information environment with cryptographic agility across EHR, PACS, laboratories, networks, identity, genomic repositories, devices and remote care.

Quantum access is more likely to arrive through universities, national laboratories and cloud providers than through a QPU in every hospital.

13. Post-Quantum Cybersecurity

Quantum risk matters before a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists because adversaries may collect encrypted information now for later decryption. Genomic, psychiatric, reproductive, paediatric, research and pharmaceutical records may retain value for decades.

  1. Create a cryptographic inventory.
  2. Classify data by confidentiality lifetime.
  3. Identify RSA and elliptic-curve dependencies.
  4. Assess vendors and connected medical devices.
  5. Require crypto-agility in procurement.
  6. Test hybrid classical/PQC protocols.
  7. Measure interoperability and performance.
  8. Plan certificate, identity and key management.
  9. Update incident response.
  10. Migrate high-risk systems in stages.

Hospitals should not assume QKD secures endpoints, accept “unhackable” claims, deploy unsupported libraries or treat migration as a one-time patch.

14. Ethics, Safety and Governance

Quantum-enabled medical technologies require analytical accuracy, precision, repeatability, reproducibility, sensitivity, specificity, reference-standard comparison, prospective external validation, subgroup analysis, usability testing and post-market surveillance. Quantum machine learning can inherit unrepresentative data, unequal subgroup performance, proxy discrimination, leakage, poor calibration, automation bias and opacity.

WHO principles concerning autonomy, safety, transparency, accountability, equity and sustainability remain applicable regardless of whether a model runs on a CPU, GPU or QPU. Early systems should remain decision support, with oversight proportionate to potential harm, uncertainty, reversibility, alternatives and evidence quality.

Shared facilities, publicly funded access, open tools, multicentre studies, scholarships and technology transfer can reduce disparities between urban and rural, public and private, and high- and low-income settings.

15. Regulation and Standards

Existing medical-device, privacy, cybersecurity and clinical-research rules continue to apply. Relevant frameworks include US FDA pathways, the EU Medical Device Regulation, UK MHRA requirements, India’s CDSCO framework, ISO 13485, ISO 14971, IEC 62304, IEC 62366, HL7 FHIR, GDPR and applicable Indian data-protection law. A quantum sensor used for diagnosis remains a medical device; a quantum-assisted diagnostic algorithm may remain software as a medical device; a cloud QPU processing health information remains subject to privacy and contractual control.

16. Workforce Requirements

Teams may include clinicians, medical physicists, biomedical engineers, quantum scientists, data scientists, software engineers, cybersecurity specialists, trial experts, health economists, regulators, ethicists and procurement professionals. Role-specific literacy is more useful than expecting every employee to become a quantum physicist.

  • Executives: maturity, investment risk, vendor claims, partnerships and security timelines.
  • Clinicians: intended use, limitations, uncertainty, validation and escalation.
  • Technical teams: hybrid computing, cloud interfaces, cryptographic migration, device integration, governance and benchmarking.

18. National Quantum Strategies

India

India’s National Quantum Mission was approved in April 2023 with ₹6,003.65 crore for 2023–24 to 2030–31. It targets quantum computing, communication, sensing, metrology, materials and high-sensitivity magnetometers, and identifies health and drug design among potential beneficiaries. Hospitals and universities can contribute through thematic-hub research, sensor validation, quantum-safe networking, use-case selection, training and drug-design partnerships.

European Union

The European Quantum Technologies Flagship supports computing, simulation, communication, sensing and metrology as a long-term research and innovation programme. Shared national and regional infrastructure can distribute capital cost, expertise, computing, validation, intellectual-property risk and workforce expense.

20. Hospital Adoption Framework

  1. Governance and literacy
  2. Problem selection
  3. Classical benchmark
  4. Controlled pilot
  5. Clinical and economic assessment
  6. Scale, revise or terminate
  1. Governance: create a multidisciplinary steering group and use-case register.
  2. Problem selection: start from measurable need, reliable data and a plausible benefit route.
  3. Benchmarking: compare accuracy, reliability, time, cost, energy, scale, integration and reproducibility.
  4. Controlled pilot: assign an owner, budget, dataset, security review, success thresholds, stop rule and oversight.
  5. Assessment: evaluate analytical validity, clinical validity, utility, workflow, equity, security, regulation and ROI.
  6. Decision: scale only after thresholds are met; otherwise revise or terminate.

22. Scenario Timeline, 2026–2046

2026–2031: Foundation and experimentation

Base: AI and GPUs remain dominant; hospitals inventory cryptography; cloud QPUs support training; OPM-MEG receives comparative evaluation; quantum-inspired scheduling pilots grow. Accelerated: logical performance improves, selected molecular work shows repeatable advantage, specialist OPM uses receive approval and government programmes fund health networks.

2031–2036: Selective integration

Base: quantum-safe protocols spread, sensing gains limited specialist uptake, hybrid research services become easier to access and regulators publish more specific guidance. Accelerated: error-corrected systems outperform practical classical methods on selected scientific workloads and validated pharmaceutical pipelines incorporate quantum subroutines.

2036–2046: Conditional expansion

Base: quantum computing remains specialised and cloud-delivered; sensors enter selected toolkits; AI and classical systems retain front-line control; PQC becomes standard. Accelerated: large error-corrected systems advance chemistry and selected optimisation, major health systems maintain dedicated access programmes and quantum-derived models contribute to biomedical digital twins.

Scenario caution: these are conditional pathways, not guaranteed dates or adoption forecasts.

23. Strategic Recommendations

Hospital leaders

  1. Strengthen data, AI, HPC and interoperability before buying quantum hardware.
  2. Begin cryptographic inventory and PQC readiness.
  3. Create a multidisciplinary steering committee.
  4. Use cloud or national facilities for early research.
  5. Require strong classical baselines and stopping rules.
  6. Treat sensing as a regulated medical-device research programme.
  7. Write crypto-agility into procurement.
  8. Measure patient, workflow, scientific and economic outcomes.

Policymakers and researchers

  • Fund translational hospital-laboratory partnerships and multicentre sensor validation.
  • Support public-sector PQC migration and shared infrastructure.
  • Publish negative results, complete methods and end-to-end timing.
  • Include clinicians and patients; pre-register clinically oriented validation where appropriate.

Vendors

  • Avoid “unhackable” and “clinically revolutionary” claims.
  • Publish reproducible benchmarks and disclose classical preprocessing.
  • Support FHIR, DICOM and hospital identity systems.
  • Provide lifecycle, cybersecurity and upgrade documentation.
  • Separate research-use-only products from approved medical devices.

24. Key Findings

  1. Quantum healthcare combines computing, sensing, communication, security and engineered materials.
  2. Quantum computing is not a universal classical replacement.
  3. AI accelerators and HPC remain hospitals’ near-term computational foundation.
  4. Quantum sensing may reach selected applications before fault-tolerant computing.
  5. OPM-MEG is among the more credible healthcare quantum-sensing areas.
  6. Quantum drug discovery remains experimental and pipeline-dependent.
  7. PQC is the most immediate strategic requirement.
  8. QKD does not replace endpoint security or identity management.
  9. Qubit counts alone do not measure useful performance.
  10. Cloud and shared access should precede on-premises QPUs.
  11. Strong classical baselines are essential.
  12. Clinical adoption requires validity, utility, safety and economic evidence.
  13. Shared infrastructure can reduce inequity.
  14. India’s National Quantum Mission creates research and workforce opportunities.
  15. Long-range futures should be scenarios, not promises.

25. Conclusion

Quantum technologies have credible long-term potential in healthcare, but progress will be gradual and uneven. Quantum sensing may achieve specialist clinical relevance sooner than universal fault-tolerant computing. Post-quantum cryptography demands immediate attention because health information can remain sensitive for decades.

Quantum computing may first influence healthcare indirectly through pharmaceutical research, molecular modelling and scientific optimisation. It is unlikely to replace conventional hospital servers or front-line clinical systems in the near term. AI, GPUs, edge computing, interoperable records and HPC will remain the principal digital foundation.

“Quantum technology should enter clinical practice through evidence, validation and accountable governance rather than technological prestige.”

The responsible strategy combines ambition with discipline: build strong classical foundations, develop literacy, migrate toward quantum-safe security, join research partnerships and require rigorous evidence before deployment.
